Do-Calculus by Hand

Two worked derivations: a confounded treatment with real numbers, and the front-door case where the confounder is never observed. Every step is followed by a refresher for readers who did math at university but no longer have the notation at their fingertips.

Notation you will need

\(P(y)\)
Probability that variable \(Y\) takes the value \(y\). Capital letter is the variable, lowercase is a concrete value.
\(P(y \mid x)\)
Conditional probability, read "probability of \(y\) given \(x\)". Look only at the cases where \(X=x\) happened, then ask how often \(Y=y\). This is seeing.
\(P(y \mid do(x))\)
Probability of \(y\) if we force \(X\) to be \(x\) for everyone, regardless of what would normally cause \(X\). This is doing. It describes a hypothetical experiment.
\(\sum_z\)
Sum over every possible value of \(Z\). With \(Z\in\{\text{young},\text{old}\}\) it means: compute the expression once for young, once for old, add the two.
\(A \perp B\)
\(A\) and \(B\) are independent: knowing one tells you nothing about the other.
\((A \perp B \mid C)\)
Independent once you know \(C\). They may look related overall, but inside each group of equal \(C\) the relationship disappears.
\(G_{\overline{X}}\)
The causal graph with every arrow pointing into \(X\) deleted. Mnemonic: the bar sits on top, like cutting the wires coming down into \(X\). This is what an intervention does: \(X\) no longer listens to its usual causes.
\(G_{\underline{X}}\)
The graph with every arrow going out of \(X\) deleted. Bar on the bottom, wires leaving \(X\) cut. Used to ask: is there any connection between \(X\) and \(Y\) other than the causal effect of \(X\)?

Reading independence off a graph (d-separation)

Every independence check below is done by looking at paths in a graph. A path is any route between two nodes following edges, ignoring arrow direction. Two variables are independent if every path between them is blocked.

There are only three kinds of junction a path can pass through at a middle node \(B\):
Chain \(A \to B \to C\): information flows through \(B\). Blocked if you condition on \(B\). (Rain makes the road wet, wet road causes accidents. Once you know the road is wet, rain tells you nothing more about accidents.)
Fork \(A \leftarrow B \to C\): \(B\) is a common cause. Also blocked if you condition on \(B\). (Age causes both treatment and recovery. Within one age group, the shared cause no longer links them.)
Collider \(A \to B \leftarrow C\): two arrows crash into \(B\). This is the opposite case: the path is blocked by default and only opens if you condition on \(B\). (Talent and luck both cause fame. Among the general population they are unrelated.)
So the checks below always reduce to: list the paths, look at each middle node, decide blocked or open.

The three rules

The goal is always the same: rewrite an expression that contains \(do(\cdot)\) until no \(do\) remains. Then it can be computed from ordinary observational data.

Each rule says: you may change the formula in a specific way, provided a certain independence holds in a certain modified graph. The rules are the permission slips; the graph checks are how you earn them.
Rule 1: insert or delete an observation
\[P(y \mid do(x), z, w) = P(y \mid do(x), w) \quad\text{if}\quad (Y \perp Z \mid X, W) \text{ in } G_{\overline{X}}\]
After the intervention on \(X\), if \(Z\) carries no information about \(Y\) (given what else you know, \(W\)), you can simply drop \(Z\) from the condition. This is ordinary conditional independence, just checked in the graph of the intervened world. Neither example below needs it, but it is part of the set.
Rule 2: swap action and observation
\[P(y \mid do(x), do(z), w) = P(y \mid do(x), z, w) \quad\text{if}\quad (Y \perp Z \mid X, W) \text{ in } G_{\overline{X}\underline{Z}}\]
When is forcing \(Z\) the same as merely seeing \(Z\)? When the only way \(Z\) and \(Y\) are connected is through \(Z\)'s own causal effect. To test that, cut the arrows leaving \(Z\) (that removes its causal effect) and check whether anything still connects \(Z\) to \(Y\). If nothing does, there is no "back door" path producing spurious correlation, and seeing equals doing.
Rule 3: insert or delete an action
\[P(y \mid do(x), do(z), w) = P(y \mid do(x), w) \quad\text{if}\quad (Y \perp Z \mid X, W) \text{ in } G_{\overline{X}\,\overline{Z(W)}}\]
If forcing \(Z\) cannot change \(Y\) at all, you can delete \(do(z)\) entirely. The test: cut the arrows into \(Z\) (as the intervention would) and check that \(Z\) and \(Y\) are disconnected.
The \(Z(W)\) detail: if some node in \(W\) is a descendant of \(Z\), you only cut arrows into those parts of \(Z\) that are not ancestors of \(W\). In both examples below \(W\) is empty, so you can read it as plain \(G_{\overline{X}\overline{Z}}\).

Example 1: a confounded treatment

\(Z\) is age (young or old), \(X\) is treatment (0 or 1), \(Y\) is recovery (0 or 1). Age influences both who gets treated and who recovers.

An arrow means "directly causes". \(Z \to X\): older people are more often treated. \(Z \to Y\): older people recover less. \(X \to Y\): the treatment's actual effect, which is what we want to measure. The trouble is that age creates a correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\) that has nothing to do with the treatment.

Step 1: split by age

\[P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid do(x), z)\; P(z \mid do(x))\]
This is just the law of total probability, \(P(y) = \sum_z P(y \mid z)\,P(z)\), applied inside the intervened world. In words: the overall recovery rate equals the recovery rate among young times the share of young, plus the same for old. Nothing causal has happened yet; we only broke the problem into two pieces, each still containing a \(do\).

Step 2: remove the action from the age distribution

\[P(z \mid do(x)) = P(z) \qquad\text{by Rule 3}\]

Check \((Z \perp X)\) in \(G_{\overline{X}}\): the only path is \(X \to Y \leftarrow Z\), a collider at \(Y\), hence blocked.

The question is: does forcing a treatment change how old people are? Obviously not, and the graph confirms it. After cutting \(Z \to X\), the only route between \(X\) and \(Z\) goes through \(Y\), where both arrows point into \(Y\). That is a collider, blocked by default, and we are not conditioning on \(Y\). So \(X\) and \(Z\) are disconnected, and the \(do(x)\) can be deleted.

Step 3: turn doing into seeing within each age group

\[P(y \mid do(x), z) = P(y \mid x, z) \qquad\text{by Rule 2}\]

Check \((Y \perp X \mid Z)\) in \(G_{\underline{X}}\): the remaining path \(X \leftarrow Z \to Y\) is a fork at \(Z\), blocked because we condition on \(Z\).

Cutting \(X \to Y\) removes the real treatment effect. If \(X\) and \(Y\) are still connected after that, the connection is spurious. Here the only leftover path runs through age, a fork. Since we are already looking within a single age group (\(z\) is in the condition), that path is blocked. Conclusion: inside one age group, treated and untreated people are comparable, so the observed recovery rate among the treated equals what forcing the treatment would give.

Result: the backdoor adjustment formula

\[P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid x, z)\; P(z)\]
Every term is now observable. Read it as a recipe: compute the recovery rate of the treated in each age group, then average those rates using the population's age mix \(P(z)\), not the age mix of the people who happened to get treated.

With numbers

young0.50.20.80.9
old0.50.80.30.4
Columns, left to right: half the population is old. 20% of young and 80% of old get treated. Recovery rates without and with treatment per age group. In each group, treatment adds exactly 10 percentage points. So we know the true answer should be +0.10.

Causal answer, using the formula:

\[P(Y{=}1 \mid do(X{=}1)) = 0.5 \cdot 0.9 + 0.5 \cdot 0.4 = 0.65\]
\[P(Y{=}1 \mid do(X{=}0)) = 0.5 \cdot 0.8 + 0.5 \cdot 0.3 = 0.55\]
First line: imagine treating everyone. Young people (half of all) would recover at 0.9, old people (other half) at 0.4. Weighted average 0.65. Second line: treat nobody, average of 0.8 and 0.3 gives 0.55. The difference 0.65 minus 0.55 is the average causal effect: +0.10. Correct.

Naive answer, comparing treated with untreated directly. First the age mix in each group, by Bayes' theorem:

\[P(\text{old} \mid X{=}1) = \frac{P(X{=}1 \mid \text{old})\,P(\text{old})}{P(X{=}1)} = \frac{0.8 \cdot 0.5}{0.8\cdot0.5 + 0.2\cdot0.5} = 0.8\]
Bayes flips the conditional: we know how likely old people are to be treated, and want how likely treated people are to be old. The denominator \(P(X{=}1)\) is the overall share treated: 0.4 from the old half plus 0.1 from the young half, so 0.5. Then 0.4 / 0.5 = 0.8. So 80% of treated people are old. By the same calculation, only 20% of untreated people are old.
\[P(Y{=}1 \mid X{=}1) = 0.8 \cdot 0.4 + 0.2 \cdot 0.9 = 0.50\]
\[P(Y{=}1 \mid X{=}0) = 0.2 \cdot 0.3 + 0.8 \cdot 0.8 = 0.70\]
Same weighted average as before, but now weighted by the age mix of each group instead of the whole population. The treated group is dominated by old people with poor recovery. Result: treated 0.50, untreated 0.70, difference \(-0.20\).

The naive comparison says the treatment harms by 20 points; the truth is it helps by 10. This sign flip is Simpson's paradox. The only difference between the two calculations is which weights are used: \(P(z)\) versus \(P(z \mid x)\).

That one substitution is the whole content of the do-calculus in this example: it proves, from the graph alone, that \(P(z)\) is the correct weight.

Example 2: the front door

In Example 1 the adjustment could have been guessed. Here it can't, because the confounder is never measured. \(X\) is smoking, \(M\) is tar in the lungs, \(Y\) is cancer, \(U\) is an unobserved genotype that influences both smoking and cancer.

Two assumptions are built into this graph. Smoking affects cancer only through tar (no direct \(X \to Y\) arrow). And the gene does not affect tar directly (no \(U \to M\) arrow). With \(U\) unmeasured, we cannot condition on it, so the trick from Example 1 is unavailable. The plan: get the effect in two hops, \(X \to M\) and \(M \to Y\), and chain them.

Step 1: effect of smoking on tar

\[P(m \mid do(x)) = P(m \mid x) \qquad\text{by Rule 2}\]

Check \((M \perp X)\) in \(G_{\underline{X}}\): the only path is \(X \leftarrow U \to Y \leftarrow M\), which has a collider at \(Y\).

Remove smoking's causal arrow and ask if anything else links smoking to tar. The only route goes up through the gene, down to cancer, and back to tar. At cancer, two arrows collide (\(U \to Y \leftarrow M\)), so the route is blocked. Smoking and tar have no shared cause, so the observed relationship is causal: seeing equals doing.

Step 2: effect of tar on cancer

\[P(y \mid do(m)) = \sum_{x} P(y \mid do(m), x)\; P(x \mid do(m))\]
Law of total probability again, this time splitting by smoking status \(x\). We will now get rid of both \(do(m)\)'s separately.
\[P(x \mid do(m)) = P(x) \qquad\text{by Rule 3}\]
Does forcing a certain amount of tar into someone's lungs change whether they smoke? No. Graph check: after cutting \(X \to M\), the only path from \(M\) to \(X\) is \(M \to Y \leftarrow U \to X\), again blocked by the collider at \(Y\). (This picture looks the same as Step 1 because \(X \to M\) is both the only arrow out of \(X\) and the only arrow into \(M\).)
\[P(y \mid do(m), x) = P(y \mid m, x) \qquad\text{by Rule 2}\]
Remove tar's causal effect on cancer. The leftover path from tar to cancer is \(M \leftarrow X \leftarrow U \to Y\): back through smoking, up through the gene. The middle node \(X\) is a chain junction, and we condition on \(X\), so the path is blocked. This is the key insight: smoking status acts as a shield against the hidden gene. Within one smoking group, the gene's influence on tar is cut off.

Putting both together:

\[P(y \mid do(m)) = \sum_{x'} P(y \mid m, x')\; P(x')\]
This is the backdoor formula from Example 1, with smoking playing the role that age played. The prime in \(x'\) is only a renamed summation index, so it doesn't get confused with the \(x\) we will intervene on in the final formula.

Step 3: chain the two hops

\[P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_m P(y \mid do(x), m)\; P(m \mid do(x))\]
Split by tar level \(m\). The second factor is already solved (Step 1). For the first factor we need two moves.
\[P(y \mid do(x), m) = P(y \mid do(x), do(m)) \qquad\text{by Rule 2}\]
Here Rule 2 is used "backwards": we replace seeing \(m\) by doing \(m\). That is allowed because the rule is an equality. Check: with arrows into \(X\) and out of \(M\) gone, \(M\) touches only \(X\), and \(X\) touches only \(M\). No path reaches \(Y\). So once smoking is forced, observing tar and forcing tar are the same thing.
\[P(y \mid do(x), do(m)) = P(y \mid do(m)) \qquad\text{by Rule 3}\]
If tar is already forced to level \(m\), does additionally forcing smoking matter for cancer? No, because smoking only acts through tar, and tar is fixed. Graph check: \(X\) has no edges left at all, completely isolated. So \(do(x)\) can be deleted.

Result: the front-door formula

\[P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_m P(m \mid x) \sum_{x'} P(y \mid m, x')\; P(x')\]
Read it as a two-stage recipe. Outer sum: how likely is each tar level \(m\) for a smoker (Step 1, directly observable). Inner sum: how much does tar level \(m\) cause cancer, estimated by comparing within smoking groups and averaging over the population's smoking rate (Step 2). Multiply and add over all tar levels. Every quantity is observational, yet the gene \(U\) never appears. The causal effect is identified without ever measuring the confounder.

The pattern

Both derivations follow the same loop: expand with the law of total probability, then for each factor pick a rule, draw the modified graph it names, and check the independence. When no \(do\) is left, you are done.

And when you can't remove all the \(do\)'s, that is informative too. Shpitser and Pearl, and independently Huang and Valtorta, proved the three rules are complete: if they fail, no amount of observational data can identify the effect for that graph. You would need an experiment, or a stronger assumption.
